1. The Water Bottle Whirlpool

When you spin a partially filled water bottle and let the water drain through a small opening (like a hole in the cap or a narrow neck), the water starts rotating and forms a funnel shape called a vortex or whirlpool.


In this project, we aim to understand:

  • How rotational motion (spinning) influences fluid behavior.
  • Why the pressure is lower near the vortex center (causing the visible “dip”).
  • The role of gravity in driving the water downward.
  • The influence of centripetal forces that keep water swirling.

By combining ideas from physics (like Bernoulli’s principle and centripetal force) and calculus (like integrals, rates of change, and differential equations), we can explain the shape, speed, and draining time of this vortex phenomenon.

2. Physical Explanation

When you spin the bottle, the water inside gains angular momentum—that means it starts moving in circles around the central axis. To maintain that circular motion, a force pulling the water inward is required. In physics, we call this the centripetal force. Without it, the water would fly outward in a straight line.

At the same time, Bernoulli’s principle tells us that faster-moving fluid regions often exhibit lower pressure. Near the center of the vortex, the water spins quickly, so the pressure is comparatively lower. This pressure difference between the faster-moving center and the slower-moving outer layers helps form the funnel’s “dip.”

As the water drains out the bottom, the height of the water in the bottle decreases. Because there is less water above, the pressure at the hole also decreases over time. The spinning flow that we see is the result of the interaction between horizontal swirling, which is controlled by rotation and pressure diffences, and vertical drainage, which is controlled by gravity.

3. Key Mathematical Tools

3.1 Bernoulli’s Principle

Bernoulli’s Principle helps relate the fluid’s velocity, pressure, and height:

$$ P + \frac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho g z = \text{constant}. $$

- \(P\) is the fluid pressure. - \(\rho\) is the fluid density (e.g., water’s density). - \(v\) is the speed of the fluid. - \(z\) is the vertical height (relative to some reference point).

In a swirling fluid, where velocity is higher at the center, we get a lower pressure there. This helps maintain the “hollow” funnel shape, because fluid moves from regions of higher pressure toward regions of lower pressure.

3.2 Centripetal Force

Water particles moving in a circle of radius \(r\) at speed \(v\) need a radial inward force:

$$ F_{\text{centripetal}} = \frac{m v^2}{r}. $$

In a continuous fluid (like water), this centripetal force is supplied by pressure gradients. Essentially, the pressure is slightly higher away from the center so it can push the water inward to maintain circular motion. If the water spins faster, \(v^2\) grows, requiring a stronger inward force—and thus a steeper pressure difference.

3.3 Volumes of Revolution

To find the volume of the funnel-shaped region (the “air space” in the vortex core), we often use calculus of solids of revolution. If \(r(h)\) is the radius of the funnel at a given height \(h\), then rotating \(r(h)\) around the vertical axis forms a solid shape. The volume of that shape is calculated by:

$$ V_{\text{vortex}} = \pi \int_{h_1}^{h_2} [r(h)]^2 \, dh. $$

This technique (the “disk” method) is crucial for quantifying how much empty space the vortex occupies and how that changes as water drains.

3.4 Torricelli’s Law & Related Rates

Torricelli’s Law helps us understand how fast water flows out of a hole when the water level is at height \(h\). It says:

$$ v_{\text{outflow}} = \sqrt{2 g h}. $$

So, the flow rate (volume per time) through the hole of area \(A_{\text{hole}}\) is:

$$ Q = A_{\text{hole}} \cdot \sqrt{2 g h}. $$

From a calculus perspective, if \(A_{\text{tank}}\) is the cross-sectional area of the water bottle, then:

$$ A_{\text{tank}} \frac{dh}{dt} = - Q = - A_{\text{hole}} \sqrt{2 g h}, $$

which is a differential equation. Solving it tells us how quickly the water height \(h\) changes over time.

3.5 Putting It All Together

In a nutshell:

  • Bernoulli & Centripetal: Determine the pressure distribution and funnel radius \(r(h)\).
  • Volumes of Revolution: Calculate the vortex’s hollow volume using integrals.
  • Torricelli’s Law: Explains how fast the bottle drains and how \(h\) decreases over time.

These principles combined enable us to predict and analyze the shape, speed, and duration of a spinning water vortex, turning a simple “spin and drain” into a rich investigation of fluid dynamics and calculus.

4. How Spinning Water Drains Faster

In many everyday “drain” experiments, you might notice that spinning water exits a container (or funnel) faster than non-spinning water. Here’s a closer look at why:

  • Creating a Stable Vortex Path:
    When the water spins, it forms a vortex with a well-defined center of rotation. By Bernoulli’s Principle (Section 3.1), fluid moves from regions of higher pressure (outer layers) to regions of lower pressure (the rapidly spinning center). This creates a “funnel” shape. In a draining scenario, that funnel provides a more direct path for air to enter from the top, preventing glugging or pressure imbalances that might slow the outflow.
  • Reduced Back-Pressure:
    As water swirls faster around the center, its velocity \(v\) (in \(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2\)) increases. That lowers \(P\) in the core (again by Bernoulli). A lower pressure zone at the hole/outlet means the water can exit more easily, accelerating the draining process compared to still water.
  • Centripetal Force and Enhanced Flow:
    From Section 3.2, a particle spinning with speed \(v\) at radius \(r\) requires \(F_{\text{centripetal}} = \frac{m v^2}{r}\). The faster swirl implies a bigger \(\tfrac{v^2}{r}\), creating a stronger pressure gradient from the outer fluid inward. This steep gradient encourages a stable vortex “column” of water to flow toward the exit.
  • Connection to Torricelli’s Law:
    Torricelli’s Law (Section 3.4) states \(v_{\text{outflow}} = \sqrt{2 g h}\). However, if the water spins rapidly, the pressure at the top of the hole can be even less than hydrostatic assumptions suggest (due to the vortex’s low-pressure center). In practice, this can slightly boost the effective outflow speed—on top of what Torricelli’s Law predicts—leading to faster draining than a purely vertical, non-rotating flow might achieve.

All in all, the spin reorganizes the fluid into a self-sustaining funnel, reduces back-pressure and air-flow disruption, and can even effectively lower the local pressure at the outflow. As a result, a spinning flow drains faster than still water.

6. Practice Problem

Try applying these ideas in a hypothetical experiment. Suppose you have a water bottle with:

  • Cross-sectional area \(A_{\text{tank}} = 0.005 \text{ m}^2\)
  • Hole area \(A_{\text{hole}} = 5\times10^{-4} \text{ m}^2\)
  • Initial water height \(h_0 = 0.30 \text{ m}\)
  • Measured vortex radius at the free surface \(r(0) \approx 0.02 \text{ m}\)

Tasks:

  1. Drainage Time: Using Torricelli’s Law, estimate how long it takes for the water height \(h\) to drop from 0.30 m to 0.05 m (just above the hole). Assume \(g = 9.8 \text{ m/s}^2\) and ignore friction.
  2. Volume of the Vortex: Suppose the vortex radius decreases linearly from \(r=0.02\) m at \(h=0.30\) m down to \(r=0\) at \(h=0.05\) m. Find the funnel volume (hint: use the disk method): $$ V = \pi \int_{h_1}^{h_2} [r(h)]^2 \, dh, $$
  3. Required Spin: If water rotates near the surface at an average speed \(v = 0.5 \text{ m/s}\) when \(r=0.02\) m, what is the centripetal force per unit mass \(F_{\text{centripetal}}/m = \frac{v^2}{r}\)? Also, comment on how greater spin speeds lead to a lower pressure at the center (by Bernoulli’s principle), thus making the vortex deeper.

7. Full Solutions & Commentary

Solution to Task 1: Drainage Time

We use Torricelli’s Law and the continuity equation:

$$ A_{\text{tank}} \frac{dh}{dt} = -A_{\text{hole}}\sqrt{2 g h}, $$

Values Given:

\( A_{\text{tank}} = 0.005\,\text{m}^2 \), \( A_{\text{hole}} = 5\times 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^2 \), and \(g=9.8\, \text{m/s}^2.\)

Rearranging, we get:

$$ \frac{dh}{dt} = - \frac{A_{\text{hole}}}{A_{\text{tank}}}\sqrt{2 g h} $$

\[\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dh}{\sqrt{h}} = -\,\frac{A_{\text{hole}}}{A_{\text{tank}}}\sqrt{2g}\,\,dt\]

We integrate from \(h=0.30\) m down to \(h=0.05\) m, and from \(t=0\) to \(t=T\):

$$ \int_{0.30}^{0.05}\frac{dh}{\sqrt{h}} = -\,\frac{A_{\text{hole}}}{A_{\text{tank}}}\sqrt{2g}\,\int_{0}^{T} dt $$

\[\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int_{0.05}^{0.30}\frac{dh}{\sqrt{h}} = \frac{A_{\text{hole}}}{A_{\text{tank}}}\sqrt{2g}\,\int_{0}^{T} dt\]

\[\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2\sqrt{h}\Big|_{0.05}^{0.30} = \frac{A_{\text{hole}}}{A_{\text{tank}}}\sqrt{2g}\,\,T\]

Substituting \( \frac{A_{\text{hole}}}{A_{\text{tank}}} = 0.1 \) and \( g=9.8 \):

$$ 2(\sqrt{0.30} - \sqrt{0.05}) = 0.1\,\sqrt{2(9.8)}\,\,T $$

\[ T \approx 1.46 \text{ s} \]

Answer: It takes about 1.46 seconds for the water level to drop from 0.30 m to 0.05 m, ignoring friction.

Solution to Task 2: Volume of the Vortex

The vortex radius is assumed to vary linearly from 0.02 m at \(h=0.30\) m to 0 at \(h=0.05\) m. The given function is:

$$ \text{slope} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{0 - 0.02}{0.05 - 0.30} = \frac{-0.02}{-0.25} = 0.08 $$

Therefore,

$$ r(h) = 0.08 \times h + b $$

To find b, we use r(0.05) = 0:

$$ 0 = 0.08 \times 0.05 + b $$

$$ \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b = 0 - 0.08 \times 0.05 = -0.004 $$

Substitute back into r(h)

$$ r(h) = 0.08 \times h - 0.004 $$

$$ \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r(h) = 0.08(h-0.05) $$

The volume of the vortex is given by:

$$ V = \pi \int_{0.05}^{0.30} \bigl[r(h)\bigr]^2 \, dh = \pi \int_{0.05}^{0.30} \bigl[0.08(h - 0.05)\bigr]^2 \, dh. $$

$$ \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V = 0.0064\,\pi \int_{0.05}^{0.30} (h - 0.05)^2 \, dh. $$

Let \( x = h - 0.05\). Then as \(h\) goes from 0.05 to 0.30, \(x\) goes from 0 to 0.25. Hence:

$$ V = 0.0064\,\pi \int_{0}^{0.25} x^2 \, dx. $$

$$ = 0.0064\,\pi\int_{0}^{0.25} x^2 \, dx $$ $$ = 0.0064\,\pi \left.\frac{x^3}{3}\right|_0^{0.25} $$ $$ = 0.0064\,\pi \frac{(0.25)^3}{3} $$ $$ = 0.0064\,\pi \frac{0.015625}{3} $$

$$ V \approx 1.0472\times10^{-4}\,\text{m}^3. $$

In liters, \(\,1\,\text{m}^3 = 1000\,\text{L}\), so:

$$ V \approx 1.0472\times10^{-4}\,\text{m}^3 = 0.10472\,\text{L} \approx 104.7\,\text{mL}. $$

Answer: The approximate volume of the funnel is \({1.05\times 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^3}\), or about 105 mL.

Solution to Task 3: Required Spin

If the water near the free surface (radius \(r=0.02\) m) rotates at speed \(v=0.5\,\text{m/s}\), the centripetal acceleration (force per unit mass) is:

$$ \frac{F_{\text{centripetal}}}{m} = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{(0.5)^2}{0.02} = \frac{0.25}{0.02} = 12.5\,\text{m/s}^2. $$

This means each kilogram of water near the radius of 0.02 m feels a radial “pull” of 12.5 newtons to maintain that circular motion. If \(v\) increases, then \(v^2\) increases quickly, so an even larger centripetal force is needed.

Comment on Deeper Vortex: According to Bernoulli’s principle, faster-moving water near the center implies lower pressure there. As the swirl speed increases, the difference in pressure from the center to the outer edges becomes greater, creating a deeper vortex.

Putting It All Together

These three tasks show how the various parts of fluid mechanics and calculus connect:

  • Torricelli’s Law (Task 1) gave us a differential equation for \(\tfrac{dh}{dt}\). Solving it revealed how quickly the water height drops over time.
  • Volumes of Revolution (Task 2) let us integrate a radius function \(r(h)\) to find the hollow volume of the vortex funnel. This is a direct application of single-variable calculus (disk/washer method).
  • Centripetal Force & Bernoulli’s Principle (Task 3) explained the link between spin speed, pressure drop, and the funnel depth. A higher rotation speed means a bigger centripetal force, which necessitates a steeper pressure gradient, in turn creating a larger “dip” in the water’s center.

In summary, the rotation of the fluid dictates pressure gradients via Bernoulli’s principle, which shape the vortex funnel. We use calculus integrals (volumes of revolution) to measure the size of that funnel, and differential equations (from Torricelli’s Law) to track how the water level changes as the bottle drains. All these aspects together form a cohesive story of how a simple spinning water bottle exhibits rich physics and mathematics in action.
